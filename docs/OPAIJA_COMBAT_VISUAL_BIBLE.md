# OPAIJA Combat and Visual Bible

## Production intent

OPAIJA action is a bold Afro-Caribbean anime-comic language grounded in Trinidad and Tobago. Stylize the energy, camera, expressions, speed, impact, and supernatural powers. Do not replace Trinidad Kalinda mechanics with generic samurai, ninja, kung-fu, bo-staff, or medieval weapon choreography.

## Kalinda scene lock

- Kalinda, Kalenda, and Calinda are accepted spellings for the complete martial, musical, ritual, and community practice.
- A gayelle is the circle or arena formed around the fighters.
- A bois is a straight hardwood fighting stick, not a rectangular board, bamboo staff, sword, spear, or katana.
- A bois fighter uses rhythmic footwork, forward-inclined balance, karay defense, feints, parries, counters, and readable above-waist attack lines.
- Gayelle scenes should integrate the shantwelle, lavway call-and-response, chorus, cutter and foolay drums, spectators, and social stakes rather than treating them as decoration.
- Mounted-bois prayer and spiritual practice must be depicted with dignity. Never turn it into a generic curse, voodoo stereotype, or random glowing sigil.

## Eight-beat fight grammar

1. Establish the gayelle, fighters, crowd, and drummers.
2. Let drum hands, lavway, breath, and footwork set the panel rhythm.
3. Present and accept the challenge.
4. Circle with the Kalinda jig and readable spatial anchors.
5. Show karay as a clear defensive pause.
6. Stage feint, parry, counter, and reaction as separate readable causes and effects.
7. Give wood-on-wood contact or a controlled landed blow a wide impact panel.
8. Return to the opponent, chorus, drummers, attendants, or crowd response.

## Multi-character panel grammar

- Use two to four focal characters per action panel unless the shot is an establishing crowd image.
- Assign each character a foreground, midground, or background anchor and a unique silhouette.
- Keep faces, costumes, skin tones, hair, powers, weapons, and gear attached to the matching character reference.
- State who attacks, who defends, who reacts, and where each weapon arc begins and ends.
- Show plausible footing, connected joints, both hands, believable grips, and one clear contact point.
- Never use a lineup or reference-sheet pose for an active fight panel.

## Anime-comic action effects

- Use foreshortening, speed lines, impact bursts, dust, grass, sweat, wood flex, splinters, drum-rhythm waves, and controlled camera shake.
- Effects must reinforce the action path and must not hide faces, hands, grips, or weapon contact.
- Reserve quiet negative space for production lettering. The image model never draws words or speech balloons.
- Sound effects are added later as editable vector typography.

## Special-power grammar

- The character bible is the only source of truth for power name, color, source point, limitations, and unlock state.
- Every power beat must declare an owner, trigger, visible effect, target reaction, cost or consequence, and continuity state after use.
- Attach power energy to the correct body, bois, or canonical object. Never float it ambiguously between fighters.
- Use a reveal sequence: anticipation, activation source, full effect, opponent/environment reaction, and recovery.
- Never add generic magic to a character whose power is inactive or not yet unlocked.

## Lettering contract

- `speaker` is metadata used to position and edit a balloon. It is never printed.
- `text` contains only words the reader should see.
- No bubble may begin with `Name:` or a character label.
- Generate clean art first. Add exact dialogue, narration, and sound effects as editable SVG, then composite the final lettered PNG.
- Keep one to three short dialogue lines per panel. Split crowded conversations across panels.

## Prompt templates

### One-on-one exchange

`[ATTACKER] advances from [ANCHOR] with [EXACT BOIS ACTION]. [DEFENDER] holds karay from [ANCHOR], then [PARRY/COUNTER]. Show rhythmic Trinidad Kalinda footwork, plausible two-ended grip, one readable contact point, gayelle crowd and drums, [CAMERA], [POWER STATE], clean art with negative space for lettering.`

### Two against one

`Stage a triangular composition. [CHARACTER A] owns foreground attack, [CHARACTER B] owns midground pressure, and [CHARACTER C] owns the defensive pivot. Keep all three silhouettes, faces, costumes, bois, grips, eyelines, and power effects distinct. Show one primary impact and two readable reactions, not three simultaneous tangles.`

### Team clash

`Use layered depth and paired action lanes. Each fighter appears exactly once and interacts with one declared opponent. Preserve canonical weapons and powers. Use crossing speed arcs without merged bodies, extra limbs, floating weapons, or hidden hands.`

### Special-power reveal

`Close anticipation on [OWNER] and [SOURCE POINT], then a wide activation panel showing [CANONICAL COLOR/EFFECT], a clear target reaction, environmental response, and the cost or recovery beat. The effect belongs only to [OWNER] and does not hide identity, grip, or contact.`

## Cultural references

- National Library and Information System Authority, Trinidad and Tobago: Carnival and Emancipation content guides.
- National Carnival Commission and Carnival Institute of Trinidad and Tobago: Calinda or Kalenda stick fighting and gayelle photo archives.
- The University of the West Indies: practitioner interviews and Old Yard cultural research.
- National Trust of Trinidad and Tobago: Piccadilly Street and Canboulay history.
- UNESCO: Trinidad and Tobago living-heritage inventory work. Do not claim Kalinda itself is UNESCO-inscribed.

Final cultural approval should include a practicing bois fighter or instructor, a shantwelle or drummer, a Trinidad Carnival researcher, and a Trinidadian visual-story editor.
