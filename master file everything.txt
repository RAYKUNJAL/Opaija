3. Historical foundation for the backstory

For the backstory, use the phrase “enslaved Africans” instead of “slaves.” That language keeps the humanity of the people first.

The historical base is strong. The National Archives of Trinidad and Tobago says the transatlantic slave trade brought approximately 44,000 enslaved Africans to Trinidad and Tobago, and its exhibitions trace the journey from Africa to plantation life, resistance, revolt, rebellion, and freedom.  

The National Archives also describes the wider African presence in Trinidad and Tobago as including people of African descent who arrived enslaved, liberated, or free during the 17th to 19th centuries.  

For stick fighting specifically, NALIS describes Kalenda/Calinda as a stick dance probably of African origin, performed with drums and chants, with fighters using sticks called bois in a circle called the gayelle. NALIS also says Canboulay and stick fights dominated Carnival in the second half of the 19th century.  

UNESCO’s martial arts archive describes Calinda/Kalinda as a Caribbean martial dance from Trinidad and Tobago, weapon-based, using sticks, accompanied by vocals and drums, and based on traditional African combat dances brought through the slave trade.  

So the story can be historically rooted while still being fictional mythology.

4. Fixing the “sticks smuggled on the ship” idea

A full mystical fighting stick being openly smuggled onto a slave ship is probably too easy and not believable. A stronger version:

The sticks were not smuggled whole.

They were broken, disguised, hidden, and spiritually encoded.

This gives you a better story.

Possible backstory

Before being forced onto ships, several African warrior lineages realized they could not carry full weapons across the ocean. So they divided their sacred staffs into ordinary-looking pieces.

The sacred bois were hidden as:

* drum pegs,
* walking cane fragments,
* carved beads,
* ship repair wood,
* pestle handles,
* prayer objects,
* hair ornaments,
* children’s toys,
* pieces of firewood,
* carved marks hidden under skin scars,
* and most importantly, songs.

The real secret was not only the wood. It was the lavway — the call-and-response chant that told future generations how to awaken the sticks.

That makes the mythology stronger:

The enemy thought they had taken every weapon.
But they did not know the weapon had been hidden inside rhythm.

This connects perfectly to Kalenda because the real Trinidad tradition already involves stick fighting, chants, drums, bois, and gayelle.
